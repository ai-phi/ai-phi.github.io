For the opening session of **ai-phi 4.0**, we’re trying a new format — and launching a new series.

**Critical Perspectives on AI** starts from a simple idea: criticising AI is one thing; understanding how criticism can help us think, design and act differently is another. The series will explore how critical perspectives can move beyond identifying problems to help formulate better questions, open alternative paths, and contribute to more constructive approaches to AI.

For this first episode, we’ll experiment with a **live podcast**, recorded in front of an audience at Sony CSL Paris.

Our first guest is **François Levin**, philosopher and author of _L’intelligence par-delà l’anthropocentrisme_. His work asks whether we can think about intelligence without taking human intelligence as its universal reference point — and what might follow if we stop treating artificial intelligence primarily as an imitation of ourselves.

His doctoral thesis, _L’intelligence artificielle au défi de ses critiques philosophiques_, takes a broader look at the philosophical critiques that have accompanied AI throughout its history. What exactly are these critiques objecting to? Have they changed as AI itself has changed? And can critique do more than point out limits — can it help us imagine and develop different forms of AI?

Together with **David Colliaux, Adel Chaibi and Aïda Elamrani**, François will discuss what we mean by intelligence in the first place; the continuing debates around computation, functionalism, embodiment and meaning; non-human forms of cognition; the possibility of a genuinely non-anthropocentric AI; and the political question of who gets to define what AI should become.

**This conversation will be held in French.** For our international community, we’re currently exploring the possibility of live English captions for the online stream. The recording will subsequently be released on YouTube with translated subtitles.

**François Levin** is a postdoctoral researcher in philosophy at Grenoble Alpes University. He received his PhD in philosophy from École Polytechnique in 2024. His research focuses on the political and epistemological issues raised by artificial intelligence. He previously worked as a rapporteur at the French Digital Council (_Conseil national du numérique_) and was a member of the Villani mission on artificial intelligence.
